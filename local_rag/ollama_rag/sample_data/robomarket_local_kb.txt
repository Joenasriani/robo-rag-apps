## Section 1: Industrial Robot Categories

### 1.1 Articulated Robots
Articulated robots are the most common type of industrial robot. They feature rotary joints (typically 4 to 6 axes) and can perform complex, multi-directional movements. Common applications include welding, painting, assembly, and material handling. Leading manufacturers include FANUC, KUKA, ABB, and Yaskawa Motoman.

Payload capacity ranges from 0.5 kg (small assembly) to 2,300 kg (heavy palletizing). Repeatability values for high-end models reach ±0.01 mm. Reach spans from 300 mm to over 4,000 mm.

### 1.2 SCARA Robots
SCARA (Selective Compliance Articulated Robot Arm) robots have 4 axes and excel at fast pick-and-place and assembly tasks in a horizontal plane. They are faster and stiffer than articulated robots for these applications. Typical payloads: 1–20 kg. Cycle time: 0.3–0.5 seconds for standard pick-and-place.

### 1.3 Delta Robots
Delta robots use three parallel arms connected to a common base. They are the fastest robot type, achieving up to 150 picks per minute. Primary use: high-speed packaging, sorting, and food processing. Payload: typically 1–15 kg. Limited workspace (within the sphere defined by arm length).

### 1.4 Collaborative Robots (Cobots)
Cobots are designed to work alongside humans without traditional safety cages. They incorporate force/torque sensing to detect contact and stop immediately. ISO/TS 15066 defines four modes of human-robot collaboration: safety-rated monitored stop, hand guiding, speed and separation monitoring, and power and force limiting. Major cobot brands: Universal Robots, FANUC CRX, ABB YuMi, KUKA iiwa, Doosan, Techman.

## Section 2: Key Performance Metrics

### 2.1 Repeatability vs. Accuracy
- **Repeatability**: How consistently a robot returns to the same programmed point. Measured as ±X mm (ISO 9283). World-class: ±0.01 mm.
- **Accuracy**: How closely the robot reaches a commanded Cartesian position. Less critical in most industrial applications where programs are taught by demonstration.

### 2.2 Payload
The maximum load the robot can carry at its wrist, including any end-of-arm tooling (EOAT). Always budget 20–30% headroom below the rated payload for dynamic loads and safety margin.

### 2.3 Reach
The maximum radial distance from the robot's base to the tool centre point (TCP). Check both maximum reach and minimum reach (dead zone near the base).

### 2.4 Cycle Time
Total time for one complete operation cycle. Influenced by payload, speed limits (especially near singularities), acceleration, and path complexity.

## Section 3: Integration Standards

### 3.1 Communication Protocols
- **EtherNet/IP**: Common in North America, especially with Allen-Bradley PLCs.
- **PROFINET**: Dominant in European Siemens-based environments.
- **Modbus TCP**: Simple, legacy-compatible, widely supported.
- **OPC UA**: Modern, platform-independent, supports complex data structures. Recommended for Industry 4.0 and IIoT integrations.

### 3.2 Robot Programming Languages
- **KRL (KUKA Robot Language)**: C-like syntax, runs on KRC controllers.
- **RAPID (ABB)**: Structured text, supports multitasking.
- **KAREL (FANUC)**: Pascal-based, supports user programs and macros.
- **URScript (Universal Robots)**: Python-like, easy to learn for cobots.

### 3.3 Simulation and Digital Twin
Common simulation platforms: RoboDK, Siemens Process Simulate, ABB RobotStudio, FANUC ROBOGUIDE, KUKA.Sim. Digital twin adoption reduces commissioning time by 30–50% on average.

## Section 4: Safety Standards

### 4.1 Applicable Standards
- **ISO 10218-1:2011**: Safety requirements for industrial robots (robot design).
- **ISO 10218-2:2011**: Safety requirements for industrial robots (integration and installation).
- **ISO/TS 15066:2016**: Collaborative robots — safety requirements.
- **EN ISO 13849-1**: Safety-related parts of control systems (Performance Levels PL a–e).
- **IEC 62061**: Functional safety of machinery — SIL (Safety Integrity Level) determination.

### 4.2 Risk Assessment
Every robot installation requires a documented risk assessment per ISO 12100. Steps: (1) determine limits of the machine, (2) identify hazards, (3) estimate risk, (4) evaluate risk, (5) reduce risk. Residual risk must be documented and communicated to the end user.
